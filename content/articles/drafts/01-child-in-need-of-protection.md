# What "child in need of protection" means in Ontario

*Legal information, not legal advice. Every case turns on its own facts. Speak to a lawyer or call Legal Aid Ontario at
1-800-668-8258.*

When a children's aid society (CAS) takes a case to court in Ontario, the first legal question is whether the child is
"in need of protection." The Child, Youth and Family Services Act, 2017 (CYFSA) answers that question with a closed list
of grounds in section 74(2). Knowing which ground the society relies on tells you what it has to prove, and what your
evidence needs to respond to.

## The grounds are specific

Section 74(2) begins: "A child is in need of protection where," and then lists clauses (a) to (o). In plain terms, the
grounds fall into these groups:

| Group | Clauses | In plain language |
|---|---|---|
| Physical harm | (a), (b) | The child **has suffered**, or there is **a risk the child is likely to suffer**, physical harm inflicted by the person in charge of the child, or resulting from that person's failure to care for, supervise or protect the child, or a pattern of neglect. |
| Sexual abuse or exploitation | (c), (d), (d.1), (d.2) | Sexual abuse or exploitation, including child sex trafficking, or a risk of it, including where the person in charge "knows or should know" of the possibility and fails to protect the child. |
| Medical treatment | (e) | The child needs treatment for physical harm or suffering and the parent does not provide it or access to it (or refuses or is unavailable to consent where the child cannot consent). |
| Emotional harm | (f), (g), (h), (i) | Emotional harm shown by serious anxiety, depression, withdrawal, self-destructive or aggressive behaviour, or delayed development, caused by the parent's actions, failure to act or pattern of neglect, or where the parent does not provide services or treatment, or a risk of either. |
| Developmental condition | (j) | A mental, emotional or developmental condition that could seriously impair development if not remedied, and the parent does not provide treatment. |
| Parent unavailable | (k) | The parent has died or is unavailable and has not made adequate provision for the child's care, or will not or cannot resume care of a child in a residential placement. |
| Children under 12 who harm others | (l), (m) | Serious harm or damage by a child under 12 where services are needed and not provided, or repeated harm encouraged by, or due to inadequate supervision of, the person in charge. |
| Consent | (n) | The parent cannot care for the child and brings the child before the court with consent. |
| Youth 16 or 17 | (o) | A prescribed circumstance or condition exists. |

Two features matter in almost every case:

- **"Has suffered" versus "risk … likely to suffer."** Several grounds are about harm that has already happened; others
  are about a *risk* that harm is *likely*. Read the society's application to see which one it relies on. A risk ground
  is about the future, so evidence of what has changed since an incident can be relevant.
- **Who caused or failed to prevent the harm.** Most grounds tie the harm to "the person having charge of the child" or
  the parent, by their actions, failure to act, or a pattern of neglect. Harm alone is usually not enough.

## What the court can order if a ground is proven

Finding a ground is not the end. Under section 101(1), the court must also be "satisfied that intervention through a
court order is necessary to protect the child in the future." If it is, it makes one of these orders "in the child's
best interests":

1. **Supervision order:** the child stays with or goes to a parent or another person, under society supervision, for
   "at least three months and not more than 12 months."
2. **Interim society care:** the child is in the society's care "for a specified period not exceeding 12 months."
3. **Extended society care:** the child stays in society care until the order ends under the Act.
4. **Consecutive orders:** interim society care followed by a return to a parent or another person under supervision,
   for "a total of 12 months" or less.

The court must also consider the society's written plan of care before making these orders (section 100; see our
article on plans of care).

## Practical steps

- Ask for the society's application and read which clause or clauses of s. 74(2) it names.
- For each clause, list what the society says happened, and what you can show in response: documents, dates,
  witnesses, and changes you have made.
- Keep your own notes separate from what others have told you, and record dates. Courts give different weight to
  first-hand observations and to second-hand reports.
- Get legal advice early. Legal Aid Ontario: 1-800-668-8258.
